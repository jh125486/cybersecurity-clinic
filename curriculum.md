# Clinic curriculum

Use one meeting per module. Open with a short lesson, spend most of the meeting on the exercise, and close with a check of the team's output. Modules 1–7 follow the Fall 2025 Canvas slide sequence. The later fieldwork block translates the syllabus and final deliverables into a club schedule.

| Module | Topic | Practice output |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Public-interest cybersecurity | Ranked threat list |
| 2 | Ethics and code of conduct | Shared conduct norms and signed acknowledgment |
| 3 | Problem diagnosis and reframing | Restaurant exercise and reframing memo |
| 4 | Asset inventory | Starter inventory and three priorities |
| 5 | Contextual brief | One-page business context brief |
| 6 | Information gathering | One-page evidence collection plan |
| 7 | Threat modeling | Scenario and short team presentation |

## 1. Public-interest cybersecurity

Explain why small organizations and civil-society groups can face serious risks with limited staff, money, and technical support. Compare a small business with a nonprofit or newsroom. Discuss phishing and account takeover, recovery from ransomware, dependence on SaaS and payment vendors, impersonation, and exposure of customer or donor data.

**Exercise.** Give participants three minutes to list five threats for each type of organization, five minutes in pairs to merge and rank their lists, and five minutes to present their top threats with an impact and likelihood rationale. Ask which operational difference changes the rankings most.

**Reading.** Shannon Vallor's ethics module, pp. 7–21 and 48–52, with the prompts on pp. 13–15 and 53 for discussion. Consult the provider's classroom-use terms. The Berkeley reading list includes the Citizen Lab, Tactical Tech, and civil-society case materials used in the course.

## 2. Ethics and code of conduct

Explain informed, voluntary consent in plain language. Cover the power imbalance between student consultants and a partner staff member, unintended harm from collecting sensitive evidence, and reliance on recommendations the organization may lack capacity to maintain. Practice explaining options and giving the partner a clear way to decline or pause.

**Exercise.** Ask each volunteer to propose one or two norms. Agree on how the team will protect client information, support respectful participation, disclose conflicts, and report an incident. Use the [conduct acknowledgment](templates.md#volunteer-conduct-acknowledgment) before any partner contact. In a scenario where a staff member offers to send a spreadsheet containing customer information, have each team explain what evidence would answer the same question with less data.

## 3. Problem diagnosis and reframing

Start with the client's proposed solution and ask what business outcome it would improve. Generate alternative explanations, look for cases where the issue does not occur, and agree on evidence that would change the diagnosis. Discuss which consulting outcome the team can support, from defining the problem through helping a partner make or sustain a change.

**In-meeting exercise.** A fictional family restaurant blames poor reviews on its Wi-Fi. Write one or two sentences for each lens. Broaden the issue to the customer experience. Narrow to where and when complaints happen. Reverse the assumption that Wi-Fi is responsible. Rephrase vague or loaded language. Compare what the proposed explanation accounts for and misses. Finish with an observation or interview that would validate the new framing.

**Reframing memo.** Write one or two pages on a fictional nonprofit with late phishing reporting, a community group using personal file-sharing accounts, or a small organization reliant on one IT volunteer. Include the original problem, a revised statement informed by at least three reframing techniques, the consulting objective it supports, and how to discuss it with the client.

## 4. Asset inventory

Inventory hardware, software and hosted services, identities, data, and third-party dependencies. Start with interviews, an escorted walkthrough, purchase records, and partner-provided administrative summaries. Capture business purpose, owner, criticality, access controls, update and backup status, exposure, and when the information was last verified.

**Exercise.** Use a fictional organization to record at least three hardware assets, three software or SaaS assets, three accounts or roles, and one dataset. Flag three risks and three feasible improvements. Explain which assets matter most to continued operations. Use the [inventory worksheet](templates.md#asset-inventory).

## 5. Contextual brief

Describe what the organization does, which workflows its staff rely on, and what constrains its security decisions. Use SAFETAG as a reference for organizing fieldwork. Use PESTLE to consider political, economic, social, technological, legal, and environmental factors. Record assumptions and questions for the partner.

**Exercise.** Assign one PESTLE factor to each group and have it identify two or three issues for a fictional bookstore. Compare how constraints would differ for a restaurant or professional office.

**Contextual brief.** Write one page that describes staffing, technology, vendor relationships, and the most relevant operating constraints. Include at least two PESTLE factors, explain how each could affect a security decision, and identify what remains unverified.

## 6. Information gathering

Use public organizational sources, interviews, partner records, and agreed observations. Separate what someone reported from what the team verified. Prioritize questions about critical workflows, service providers, access ownership, backups, and how staff report suspected problems.

**Exercise.** Give each team a fictional business profile. Have it identify two kinds of public sources and draft three questions for an owner or staff member. Discuss which questions the team can remove without weakening its assessment.

**Plan.** Draft one page covering three to five public sources or source types, five to seven interview questions, and what evidence the team expects from each. List the collection owner, the sensitivity of the expected evidence, and how that evidence will be handled. Use the [information gathering plan](templates.md#information-gathering-plan).

## 7. Threat modeling

Use personas to connect likely risks to business operations. Describe motivation, capability, and the kind of access the persona might already have. For each scenario, articulate a plausible precondition, likely business impact, evidence needed, and the control or process that would reduce exposure.

**Exercise.** Draw a fictional persona and give a short presentation. For example, a former volunteer may still have a staff account after leaving. Explain what process weakness would make that possible, which business information could be affected, and how to check whether offboarding closes accounts. Record the result in the [risk register](templates.md#risk-register).

## Fieldwork and closeout

- **Baseline review.** Extend the inventory into a discussion of access management, device upkeep, backups, communications, and incident readiness. Use a fictional case to rehearse before collecting partner evidence.
- **Coaching.** Practice explaining account protections and how to recognize and report suspicious messages or harassment in a discussion or tabletop.
- **Weekly check-ins.** From scope approval through report review, each team reports progress, evidence gaps, risks requiring escalation, decisions, and the next owner.
- **Final review.** Use the [client memorandum and presentation templates](templates.md#client-memorandum). Ask the partner to identify the first action they can take, the owner, and the date they will revisit it.
